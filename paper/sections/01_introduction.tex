\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The single-velocity six-equation two-phase model with relaxation \cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022} is widely
used for compressible liquid--gas and liquid--vapour flows: cavitation in hydraulic machinery and fuel injectors,
flashing and evaporation fronts and the rapid depressurisation of pressurised water in nuclear reactor safety,
underwater explosions, and the shock loading of condensed materials and mixtures
\cite{SaurelPetitpasAbgrall2008,Bartak1990,PelantiShyue2019,Chiapolino2017,DeLorenzo2018,DeLorenzo2019,Schmidmayer2020,SaurelPantano2018}.
Two features make these flows difficult. First, the Mach number varies by orders of magnitude within one flow: a
liquid at a few metres per second is in the incompressible regime, while gas, vapour pockets or detonation products
in the same domain may be transonic or carry strong shocks. Second, the phases exchange momentum, heat and mass on
time scales from much slower to much faster than the flow, so that one computation contains frozen, finite-rate and
equilibrium regions, and phase transition through metastable states.

Diffuse-interface models describe such flows with one set of equations throughout the domain, pure fluids, interfaces
and mixtures alike \cite{SaurelAbgrall1999a,AbgrallKarni2001,SaurelPantano2018}. They derive from the Baer--Nunziato seven-equation model
\cite{BaerNunziato1986}, where each phase has its own velocity and pressure. Velocity relaxation gives the two-pressure
six-equation model, additional pressure relaxation the five-equation model of Kapila et al.\
\cite{Kapila2001,MurroneGuillard2005,AllaireClercKokh2002}, and further thermal and chemical relaxation the
four-equation $pT$ model and the homogeneous equilibrium model \cite{FlattenLund2011,Lund2012,Pelanti2022}. Saurel, Petitpas and
Berry \cite{SPB2009} proposed computing interfaces and mixtures with the six-equation model and stiff pressure
relaxation: its frozen sound speed is monotone in the volume fraction, its volume-fraction equation contains only the
pressure work, and the mixture total energy, redundant for smooth flows, is added as a seventh, ``+1'' equation to
recover the conservative mixture energy at shocks. Pelanti and Shyue \cite{PelantiShyue2014} made the approach
mixture-energy consistent and added heat and mass transfer \cite{SaurelPetitpasAbgrall2008,Zein2010}, and Pelanti
\cite{Pelanti2022} extended the relaxation procedures to arbitrary rates and to the Noble--Abel stiffened-gas
\cite{LeMetayerSaurel2016} and Mie--Gr\"uneisen equations of state.

A known difficulty of the six-equation model is its behaviour at shocks in mixtures: the model is non-conservative,
its shock relations \cite{SaurelGavrilyukRenaud2003} are not those of the numerical shock layer, and the energy
deposited in the layer is shared between the phases in a scheme-dependent way. Saurel et al.\ \cite{SPB2009} showed
that the computed post-shock state then deviates from the five-equation solution for strong shocks, and corrected it
with an artificial heat exchange in the shock layer whose coefficient must be calibrated for each mixture
\cite{Petitpas2007}. A second difficulty is stiffness of two kinds: the acoustic waves, fast compared with the flow at
low Mach numbers, and the mechanical, thermal and chemical relaxation, whose rates range from zero to infinity and may
differ by orders of magnitude between the three processes. The methods above
\cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022,DeLorenzo2018} and the seven-equation solver of Sirianni and Re
\cite{SirianniRe2026} are explicit Godunov-type schemes with HLLC, Roe or wave-propagation solvers, which integrate the
relaxation separately after a hyperbolic step restricted by the acoustic CFL condition. Since a small amount of liquid
raises the frozen sound speed of a mixture to nearly the liquid value, a flow at a few metres per second is then advanced
with the time step of a wave at more than a kilometre per second, and the upwind dissipation, which scales with the
sound speed, destroys the low-Mach asymptotics of the pressure
\cite{GuillardViozat1999,GuillardMurrone2004,Dellacherie2010}. Preconditioning restores the correct scaling of the
dissipation \cite{Turkel1987,Pelanti2017} and, with implicit time integration, removes the time step restriction
\cite{LeMartelot2013}, but at the price of large nonlinear systems coupling all variables, whose solution is costly and
whose convergence is difficult to control. Semi-implicit methods avoid both difficulties by treating only the stiff terms
implicitly and the nonlinear transport explicitly.

Pressure-based semi-implicit methods go back to Harlow and Welch \cite{HarlowWelch1965} and Harlow and Amsden
\cite{HarlowAmsden1971}, with fundamental contributions by Casulli and Greenspan \cite{CasulliGreenspan1984}, Klein
\cite{Klein1995}, Munz et al.\ \cite{Munz2003} and Park and Munz \cite{ParkMunz2005}. Conservative, shock-capturing
versions split the flux into a convective part with eigenvalues given by the flow velocity and a pressure part carrying
the acoustic waves \cite{ZhaBilgen1993,LiouSteffen1993,ToroVazquez2012}. Dumbser and Casulli \cite{DumbserCasulli2016}
combined the Toro--V\'azquez-Cend\'on splitting with an implicit pressure sub-system for general equations of state;
this system is only mildly nonlinear, the nonlinearity being confined to the diagonal through the equation of state,
and is solved by the nested Newton method of Casulli and Zanolli \cite{CasulliZanolli2012}. Higher-order and all-Mach finite volume and
discontinuous Galerkin extensions followed
\cite{TavelliDumbser2017,Boscheri2020,BoscheriPareschi2021,BoscheriTavelli2022,Boscarino2022,Orlando2022}, and in ideal
magnetohydrodynamics the Alfv\'en and magnetosonic waves were successively removed from the explicit step
\cite{Dumbser2019,Fambri2021,BoscheriThomann2024,Dematte2024}, with a pressure-based formulation for a general equation
of state and pressure positivity enforced inside the implicit solve by Chen et al.\ \cite{Chen2026}. Implicit--explicit (IMEX)
Runge--Kutta schemes \cite{Ascher1997,PareschiRusso2005} provide second- and higher-order time integration; their
stiffly accurate, L-stable members preserve relaxation limits
\cite{PareschiRusso2005,BoscarinoRusso2009,BoscarinoPareschiRusso2017}. A scheme that reduces, as the Mach number tends
to zero, to a consistent discretisation of the incompressible limit with a Mach-independent time step is asymptotic
preserving \cite{Jin1999,KlainermanMajda1981}; such all-speed schemes have been analysed for the Euler equations
\cite{DegondTang2011,HaackJinLiu2012,Cordier2012,Noelle2014,Dimarco2018,Boscheri2020}. These developments concern mainly
single-phase flows.

For diffuse-interface two-phase models, pressure-based, all-Mach and IMEX schemes are still few: they exist for weakly
compressible flows under a Baer--Nunziato type model \cite{ReAbgrall2022}, low-Mach flows with mass transfer
\cite{Demou2022}, a two-phase model with phasic pressures \cite{MalusaAlaia2024}, a two-fluid single-temperature model
\cite{Lukacova2024} and, with an all-regime Lagrange-projection scheme that treats acoustics and phase transition
implicitly, homogeneous two-phase models \cite{ChalonsGirardinKokh2017}; convection--acoustics splittings have been
derived for the Baer--Nunziato \cite{TokarevaToro2017} and Kapila \cite{tenEikelder2017} models. Battisti and Boscheri
\cite{BattistiBoscheri2025} designed a linearly implicit, asymptotic preserving and well-balanced scheme for the
seven-equation model, with explicit Rusanov convective fluxes, centrally discretised implicit pressure terms and the
stiff relaxation sources solved separately. Closest to the present work, Schropff, Petitpas and Daniel
\cite{Schropff2026} derived a Zha--Bilgen type splitting of the single-velocity two-pressure model and a one-dimensional
semi-implicit scheme that solves the pressure sub-system by Picard iterations and corrects the phase internal energies
to conserve total energy; in their computations the pressure relaxation is instantaneous and applied after the IMEX
step. These schemes thus handle the two stiffnesses separately: the relaxation is either restricted to instantaneous
pressure equilibrium or solved in a separate step. Outside diffuse-interface models, interphase heat and mass transfer
enter the implicit pressure step of semi-implicit two-fluid system codes such as RELAP5 \cite{RELAP5}, where they are
linearised in time and eliminated, with the junction velocities, to leave one pressure equation per volume. Similarly,
the all-Mach volume-of-fluid solver of Saade, Lohse and Fuster \cite{Saade2023} solves pressure and temperature, with
heat conduction, as one coupled system.

In this work we treat both stiffnesses in a single implicit step. The pressure work and the three relaxation processes,
with finite or infinite rates under the time-scale ordering of Pelanti \cite{Pelanti2022}, enter one local closure per
cell, which reduces to one scalar equation, in the cell pressure or, for finite mechanical relaxation, in the volume
increment. The implicit step is a nonlinear system for the cell pressures whose nonlinearity from the equations of state
and the relaxation is local to each cell, the cells being coupled through the face velocities as in the mildly nonlinear
systems of \cite{DumbserCasulli2016,CasulliZanolli2012}; its Jacobian is a sparse three-point matrix, five-point in 2D,
and it is solved by Newton's method. The closure uses the equations of state without linearisation, and each finite-rate
relaxation is one backward-Euler step over the implicit step, which tends to the equilibrium closure as the rate tends
to infinity and is the closure without that process at zero rate: one unified formula covers every rate. The
arbitrary-rate procedures of Pelanti \cite{Pelanti2022} and of Sirianni and Re \cite{SirianniRe2026} integrate the
relaxation equations with coefficients frozen over each step or sub-step and, in the infinite-rate limit, reach
equilibrium only approximately or emulate it with large finite rates, respectively; Sirianni and Re also treat
concurrent finite-rate mechanical and thermo-chemical relaxation, which the ordering of Pelanti used here excludes. Our
scheme is asymptotic preserving with respect to both stiffnesses: at fixed mesh, under non-degeneracy assumptions and for
data on the equilibrium manifold it converges to the corresponding equilibrium scheme as each relaxation rate tends to
infinity, and it reduces to the scheme without that relaxation as the rate vanishes. It is formally asymptotic
preserving in the low Mach number limit, with a time step set by a CFL condition on the material speed only, whatever
the Mach number and the relaxation rates.

The second ingredient concerns shocks: the difference between the conservative and non-conservative mixture internal
energies, which at a shock is the scheme's dissipation, is distributed between the phases according to the shock
relations of the model \cite{SaurelGavrilyukRenaud2003}, without a calibrated correction. We show that for weak shocks
the share consistent with these relations is unique, compute for every shock strength the constant share for which the
shock profile of the relaxed model ends on the Hugoniot of each phase, and distribute the remaining discretisation error
in the unique linear proportion that keeps both phases admissible whenever possible.

The discretisation follows a Zha--Bilgen splitting \cite{ZhaBilgen1993}: the explicit step transports all quantities
with the flow velocity by the path-conservative Rusanov scheme \cite{Pares2006,Castro2006} with material-speed
dissipation, and advects the volume fraction entirely, with a fluctuation that keeps the volume, mass and energy of a
trace phase consistent; the implicit step carries the pressure, its work on each phase and an incremental flux of the
conservative transported quantities. The central implicit operator is supplemented by Laplace dissipation of the
pressure and velocity with acoustic coefficients, the upwind terms of the acoustic Riemann solution, weighted by the
deficit of the backward-Euler viscosity relative to the upwind one, so that it vanishes at low Mach numbers; it is first
order at local extrema and second order in smooth regions \cite{Jameson1995}. The stiffly accurate LSDIRK2 scheme
\cite{BoscarinoFilbetRusso2016} gives second order in time. The scheme applies to stiffened-gas and Noble--Abel
stiffened-gas phases with all three relaxation processes and to Mie--Gr\"uneisen phases with instantaneous mechanical
relaxation, in one and two dimensions.

We further prove that the scheme preserves moving contacts between
stiffened-gas or Noble--Abel stiff\-ened-gas phases, at uniform temperature when thermal or chemical relaxation is active.
It is validated on twenty-two problems in one and two space dimensions, counting the single- and two-phase Taylor--Green
vortices separately: the convergence and low-Mach tests adapted from Battisti and Boscheri \cite{BattistiBoscheri2025},
single-phase Riemann problems at low and high Mach numbers and two low-Mach advection problems from Schropff et al.\
\cite{Schropff2026}, and the interface, shock, cavitation, evaporation and depressurisation problems of
\cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022}.
