\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The single-velocity six-equation two-phase model with relaxation \cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022} is widely
used for compressible liquid--gas and liquid--vapour flows: cavitation in hydraulic machinery and fuel injectors,
flashing and evaporation fronts and the rapid depressurisation of pressurised water in nuclear reactor safety,
underwater explosions, and the shock loading of condensed materials and mixtures
\cite{SaurelPetitpasAbgrall2008,Bartak1990,PelantiShyue2019,Chiapolino2017,DeLorenzo2018,DeLorenzo2019,Schmidmayer2020,SaurelPantano2018,SirianniRe2026}.
It is the diffuse-interface model obtained from the Baer--Nunziato model \cite{BaerNunziato1986} by relaxing the
velocities: each phase keeps its own pressure, and mechanical, thermal and chemical relaxation drive the phases
towards equal pressures, temperatures and Gibbs free energies, so that the five-equation model of Kapila et al.\
\cite{Kapila2001}, the $pT$ model and the homogeneous equilibrium model \cite{FlattenLund2011,Pelanti2022} are
recovered as limits. Unlike the five-equation model, its frozen sound speed is monotone in the volume fraction and its
volume fractions remain positive in strong expansions and compressions; the mixture total energy, redundant for smooth
flows, is added as a seventh equation to restore the conservative mixture energy at shocks \cite{SPB2009}. Pelanti and
Shyue \cite{PelantiShyue2014} made the approach mixture-energy-consistent and added heat and mass transfer
\cite{SaurelPetitpasAbgrall2008,Zein2010}, and Pelanti \cite{Pelanti2022} extended the relaxation procedures to arbitrary
rates and to the Noble--Abel stiffened-gas \cite{LeMetayerSaurel2016} and Mie--Gr\"uneisen equations of state.
Two features make these flows difficult. First, the Mach number varies by orders of magnitude within one flow: a
liquid moving at a few metres per second is in the incompressible regime, while the gas, a vapour pocket or a
detonation product in the same domain may be transonic or carry strong shocks. Second, the phases exchange
momentum, heat and mass on time scales that range from much slower to much faster than the flow itself, so that
the same computation contains frozen, finite-rate and equilibrium regions, and phase transition through
metastable states.

To our knowledge, no IMEX scheme exists for the six-equation model with mechanical, thermal and chemical relaxation.
In this model two kinds of stiffness meet: the acoustic waves, fast compared with the flow at low Mach numbers, and the
relaxation processes, whose rates range from zero to infinity and may differ by orders of magnitude between the three
processes. Existing methods treat them separately. Explicit schemes
\cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022,DeLorenzo2018,SirianniRe2026} integrate the relaxation in a step of its own
after a hyperbolic step restricted by the acoustic CFL condition; since a small amount of liquid raises the frozen
sound speed of a mixture to nearly the liquid value, a flow at a few metres per second is then advanced with the time
step of a wave moving at more than a kilometre per second, and the upwind dissipation, which scales with the sound
speed, destroys the low-Mach asymptotics of the pressure \cite{GuillardViozat1999,Dellacherie2010}. The semi-implicit
two-phase schemes either consider only instantaneous pressure relaxation \cite{Schropff2026} or solve the relaxation
sources in a separate step \cite{BattistiBoscheri2025}. A further difficulty concerns shocks in mixtures: the
six-equation model is non-conservative, its shock relations \cite{SaurelGavrilyukRenaud2003} are not those of the
scheme's numerical shock layer, and the energy deposited in the layer is shared between the phases in a way that
depends on the scheme. Saurel et al.\ \cite{SPB2009} showed that the computed post-shock state then deviates from
the five-equation solution for strong shocks, and corrected it with an artificial heat exchange in the shock layer
whose coefficient must be calibrated for each mixture \cite{Petitpas2007}.

Semi-implicit methods treat implicitly only the terms responsible for the stiffness and keep the nonlinear transport
explicit. Pressure-based methods go back to \cite{HarlowWelch1965,HarlowAmsden1971,CasulliGreenspan1984,Klein1995,Munz2003};
conservative, shock-capturing versions split the flux into a convective part, whose eigenvalues are given by the flow
velocity, and a pressure part carrying the acoustic waves \cite{ZhaBilgen1993,LiouSteffen1993,ToroVazquez2012}, and
with a general equation of state the implicit pressure system is only mildly nonlinear \cite{DumbserCasulli2016,CasulliZanolli2012}.
Higher-order and all-Mach extensions followed in finite volume and discontinuous Galerkin frameworks
\cite{TavelliDumbser2017,Boscheri2020,BoscheriPareschi2021}, and in magnetohydrodynamics
\cite{Dumbser2019,Fambri2021,Chen2026}. Time integration of second and higher order is provided by implicit--explicit
(IMEX) Runge--Kutta schemes \cite{Ascher1997,PareschiRusso2005}, whose stiffly accurate, L-stable members preserve
relaxation limits \cite{PareschiRusso2005,BoscarinoRusso2009,BoscarinoPareschiRusso2017}, and by their semi-implicit
variants \cite{BoscarinoFilbetRusso2016}. A scheme that reduces, as the Mach number tends to zero, to a consistent
discretisation of the incompressible limit with a time step independent of the Mach number is asymptotic preserving
\cite{Jin1999,KlainermanMajda1981}; such all-speed schemes have been analysed for the Euler equations in
\cite{DegondTang2011,HaackJinLiu2012,Dimarco2018,Boscheri2020}. For two-phase
flows, pressure-based and IMEX schemes have been developed for Baer--Nunziato and Kapila type models
\cite{ReAbgrall2022,TokarevaToro2016,tenEikelder2017,Lukacova2024,MalusaAlaia2024}. Battisti and Boscheri
\cite{BattistiBoscheri2025} designed a linearly implicit, asymptotic preserving and well-balanced scheme for the
seven-equation model, with explicit Rusanov fluxes for the convective terms, central implicit discretisation of the
pressure terms and the stiff relaxation sources solved in a separate step. Closest to the present work, Schropff,
Petitpas and Daniel \cite{Schropff2026} derived a Zha--Bilgen type splitting of the single-velocity two-pressure model
and a semi-implicit scheme in which the pressure sub-system is solved by Picard iterations and the phase internal
energies are corrected to conserve total energy; their scheme is restricted to one space dimension and instantaneous
pressure relaxation, and the dissipation of its explicit step is a multiple of the material speed with a coefficient
chosen for each test.

In this work we treat both stiffnesses in a single implicit step. The pressure work and the three relaxation processes,
at any combination of finite and infinite rates, enter one local closure per cell, which reduces to a scalar equation
for the cell pressure; the implicit step is then a nonlinear system for the cell pressures, mildly nonlinear in the
sense of \cite{DumbserCasulli2016,CasulliZanolli2012}, with a three-point (five-point in 2D) Jacobian, solved by a Newton
method whose iteration count is observed to be independent of the Mach number. The resulting scheme is asymptotic
preserving with respect to both stiffnesses: it converges to the corresponding equilibrium scheme as each relaxation
rate tends to infinity, reduces to the scheme without that relaxation as the rate vanishes, and is formally asymptotic
preserving in the low Mach number limit, with a time step set by a CFL condition on the material speed only, whatever
the Mach number and the relaxation rates.

The second ingredient is the treatment of shocks. The difference between the conservative and the non-conservative
mixture internal energy, which at a shock is the dissipation of the scheme, is distributed between the phases
according to the shock relations of the model \cite{SaurelGavrilyukRenaud2003}, instead of by a calibrated correction.
We show that for weak shocks the share consistent with these relations is unique, compute for every shock strength the
constant share for which the shock profile of the relaxed model ends on the Hugoniot of each phase, and distribute the
remaining discretisation error in the unique linear proportion that keeps both phases admissible.

The discretisation follows a splitting in the manner of Zha and Bilgen \cite{ZhaBilgen1993}: all quantities are
transported explicitly by the path-conservative Rusanov scheme \cite{Pares2006,Castro2006} with the material speed as
dissipation speed, the volume fraction being advected entirely in this step, and the implicit step carries the
pressure, its work on each phase and an incremental flux of the conservative transported quantities. Implicit Laplace
dissipation of the pressure and the velocity, the upwind terms of the acoustic Riemann solution, is weighted so that it
vanishes in the low Mach number regime and limited to second order in smooth regions. Second order in time is obtained
with the LSDIRK2 scheme \cite{BoscarinoFilbetRusso2016}; stiffened-gas, Noble--Abel stiffened-gas and Mie--Gr\"uneisen
equations of state are treated, in one and two space dimensions.

Besides the relaxation limits and the low Mach number limit, we prove that the scheme preserves moving contacts (with
equal phase temperatures when thermal relaxation is active) and the temperature of each phase under transport. The
scheme is validated on twenty-two problems in one and two space dimensions (counting the single- and two-phase
Taylor--Green vortices separately): the convergence and low-Mach tests of \cite{BattistiBoscheri2025}, single-phase
Riemann problems at low and high Mach numbers and a low-Mach interface advection from \cite{Schropff2026}, and the
interface, shock, cavitation, evaporation and depressurisation problems of \cite{SPB2009,PelantiShyue2014,Pelanti2022}.
